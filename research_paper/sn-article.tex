\abstract{Accurate forecasting of energy commodity prices --- particularly West Texas Intermediate (WTI) crude oil and Henry Hub natural gas --- remains a persistent methodological challenge, owing to their intrinsic price volatility, pronounced sensitivity to geopolitical disruptions, and structurally complex dependence on macroeconomic cycles. This paper examines whether macroeconomic indicators from the Federal Reserve Economic Data (FRED) database and news-based signals derived from the Global Database of Events, Language, and Tone (GDELT) improve forecasting performance relative to standard price-only baselines. The analysis covers a full forecasting pipeline, including stationarity testing, seasonal decomposition, feature transformation, and multivariate modeling with both classical statistical methods and neural architectures with exogenous inputs. We tuned hyperparameters with Optuna using 30 trials for each model configuration. We evaluated model performance with Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) and further checked it with walk-forward backtesting. Empirical results indicate that, while point-level forecast accuracy remained constrained across all configurations, the proposed multivariate framework exhibited a meaningful capacity to identify directional price movements and cyclical turning points that univariate baseline models systematically failed to detect. Collectively, these findings suggest that heterogeneous data fusion holds substantive promise for US energy price modeling, while simultaneously underscoring the practical limitations of aggregated news-derived features in high-frequency commodity forecasting contexts.}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec1}

Energy commodity prices --- most prominently crude oil and natural gas --- serve as first-order indicators of global economic activity and are deeply embedded in the transmission mechanisms of inflation, industrial output, and monetary policy~\cite{baumeister2016,hamilton2009,kilian2009}. Changes in these prices affect transportation costs, household energy bills, production decisions, and inflation expectations, so even modest improvements in forecasting can matter for policymakers, firms, and investors~\cite{baumeister2016,hamilton2009}. At the same time, forecasting is difficult because price movements reflect a shifting mix of supply imbalances, geopolitical disruptions, business-cycle conditions, and financial-market behavior~\cite{hamilton2003,kilian2009}.

Energy-price forecasting has therefore been studied through both classical time-series models and more flexible neural architectures, but no single model family consistently dominates across commodities and market regimes~\cite{box1976,hyndman2018,hochreiter1997,sainath2015}. In parallel, prior work suggests that macroeconomic context and news-driven information may add signal beyond price history alone, motivating comparative evaluation of structured external features alongside standard univariate baselines~\cite{kilian2009,tetlock2007,shapiro2022,leetaru2013}.

Still, work that evaluates price history, macroeconomic variables, and news-based signals within one shared forecasting framework is relatively limited~\cite{baumeister2016,shapiro2022}. Univariate models capture persistence in the series but ignore outside information. Multivariate models are more informative, but their gains are not always stable or easy to compare across studies~\cite{box1976,baumeister2016,hyndman2018}. Many papers also focus on one commodity, one model family, or one external data source at a time. That makes it hard to judge the relative value of macroeconomic indicators and event-driven news under the same evaluation design. To address that issue, we build a reproducible forecasting framework that compares classical statistical models and hybrid neural architectures across four settings: univariate, macro-only, news-only, and combined features. We evaluate all models with the same walk-forward validation logic and the same error metrics.

The main expectation is that external information should be most useful when macroeconomic indicators and news-derived signals are considered together rather than separately. More specifically, we test whether macroeconomic variables improve upon univariate baselines, whether GDELT-derived signals contribute additional information, and whether the joint feature space produces stronger directional performance than either feature group on its own.

This paper makes three contributions. First, it compares univariate and multivariate forecasting strategies for both WTI crude oil and Henry Hub natural gas within a single pipeline. Second, it separates the contribution of macroeconomic variables, news-derived variables, and their combination so that the role of each feature group is easier to interpret. Third, it pairs standard forecast metrics with walk-forward backtesting to check whether apparent improvements persist beyond a single held-out period.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec2a}

The literature on energy-price forecasting can be organized into three broad strands: classical econometric work on price dynamics and structural shocks, deep-learning studies that emphasize nonlinear prediction, and news- or text-based work that brings in external information. Because this paper compares macroeconomic and GDELT-derived inputs inside one common pipeline, the most relevant prior studies are not just those with the lowest reported errors, but those that clarify what each source of information adds, under what evaluation design, and where the evidence is still thin.

\subsection*{Classical energy-price and macroeconomic forecasting studies}
Early empirical work made it clear that oil-price movements cannot be treated as a purely mechanical autoregressive process. Hamilton~\cite{hamilton2003} showed that oil shocks emerge through distinct economic channels and are tied to broader macroeconomic adjustment. Kilian~\cite{kilian2009} pushed this further by separating oil-price fluctuations into demand- and supply-driven shocks, showing that price changes do not all have the same origin or implication. These studies matter here because they justify using exogenous information instead of relying only on lagged prices. At the same time, they were not designed as broad forecasting comparisons across multiple model classes, nor do they ask whether modern event-based data improve practical out-of-sample prediction for both crude oil and natural gas.

Baumeister and Kilian~\cite{baumeister2016} offered a useful cautionary perspective. Their review of four decades of oil-price behavior argues that oil prices remain hard to predict because structural relationships shift over time and because apparent forecasting gains often fade across regimes. That point directly motivates the walk-forward backtesting used in this paper. Even so, their article is a macroeconomic synthesis rather than a side-by-side empirical comparison of classical, neural, macro-only, news-only, and combined-feature models within one reproducible framework.

Within the time-series tradition, Box and Jenkins~\cite{box1976} established the foundations of ARIMA-type forecasting, while Durbin and Koopman~\cite{durbin2012} formalized state-space methods for modeling latent dynamics and measurement structure. These references remain central because they justify ARIMA-, SARIMA-, and SARIMAX-type baselines before moving to more flexible models. What they do not answer is the specific question considered here: once those baselines are in place, do macroeconomic indicators and event-derived news features actually improve forecasting?

A closer benchmark for this project is the use of macroeconomic covariates in energy-price prediction. FRED itself is data infrastructure rather than a forecasting study~\cite{fred2025}, but the variables it distributes - industrial production, exchange rates, inflation proxies, and volatility measures - are exactly the kinds of structured signals commonly used to contextualize commodity prices. The limitation of this macro-focused literature is that it usually stops there. It seldom asks how macroeconomic variables perform alongside event-driven external signals, and it rarely studies both WTI crude oil and Henry Hub natural gas under the same evaluation design.

\subsection*{Deep-learning studies for commodity and financial forecasting}
A second line of work asks whether neural architectures can outperform classical statistical models when the underlying process is nonlinear. Hochreiter and Schmidhuber~\cite{hochreiter1997}, together with Gers et al.~\cite{gers2000}, developed the LSTM architecture to handle long-range temporal dependence more effectively than standard recurrent networks. Those papers justify the use of LSTM here, but they are architecture papers rather than direct commodity-forecasting benchmarks.

Application-oriented studies report a more mixed picture. Cen and Wang~\cite{cen2019} used an LSTM-based framework for crude-oil prediction and argued that deep learning can help when prior knowledge and transferred representations are incorporated. Busari and Lim~\cite{busari2021} compared AdaBoost-LSTM and AdaBoost-GRU models for crude oil and found gains over simpler baselines. Kim and Won~\cite{kim2018}, working on stock-index volatility rather than energy markets, likewise showed that hybrid deep-learning pipelines can outperform narrower benchmarks when complementary temporal representations are combined. Taken together, these studies support the idea that neural models can exploit nonlinear structure.

At the same time, those studies do not fully address the question pursued here. Most focus on one commodity or one financial series rather than comparing two energy commodities with different market structures. They also tend to emphasize point-forecast accuracy without pairing it with walk-forward backtesting. Finally, they usually do not separate the contribution of macroeconomic variables from that of event-driven news within the same experimental design. This paper therefore builds on the deep-learning literature, but asks a broader comparative question: not just whether LSTM-type models can work, but when they help relative to classical models and which kinds of exogenous information matter most.

Hybrid CNN--LSTM architectures are especially relevant in this context. LeCun et al.~\cite{lecun1998} established the broader logic of convolutional feature extraction, and Sainath et al.~\cite{sainath2015} showed how convolutional and recurrent components can be combined to exploit both local and sequential structure. That architecture motivates the CNN--LSTM models used here, especially in multivariate settings where short-run feature interactions may matter. Still, prior hybrid-architecture studies leave open the question most important for this paper: whether these models benefit more from macroeconomic context, from news-derived signals, or from the joint feature space.

\subsection*{News, sentiment, and event-driven external signals}
A third and increasingly important literature examines whether text or event information improves market prediction. Tetlock~\cite{tetlock2007} showed that media content contains economically relevant information and that textual pessimism is associated with market behavior. Li et al.~\cite{li2014} then showed that news sentiment can improve stock-return prediction, suggesting that narrative information carries incremental value beyond prices alone. More recently, Shapiro et al.~\cite{shapiro2022} developed a systematic approach to measuring news sentiment and showed that such indices can recover interpretable macro-financial information. These studies matter directly here because they support the idea that event-driven information may help forecast energy prices, or at least improve directional interpretation.

However, much of this literature focuses on equity markets, general macro-financial conditions, or custom text corpora rather than monthly forecasting of WTI crude oil and Henry Hub natural gas. Many text-based approaches also depend on full-text processing or hand-built sentiment dictionaries, which can make replication harder. Our setup differs by using GDELT, a structured event database that provides standardized information on event type, tone, and geopolitical context at scale.

Leetaru and Schrodt~\cite{leetaru2013} introduced GDELT as a global event database that converts large news flows into structured records. Goldstein~\cite{goldstein1992} provided the conflict--cooperation scale that underlies one of the key event-intensity measures used here. Baker et al.~\cite{baker2016} also showed the broader predictive usefulness of text-derived uncertainty measures, reinforcing the view that narrative and event information can encode economically relevant variation. What these studies do not show is whether GDELT-derived features outperform or complement macroeconomic variables in energy-price forecasting under a controlled comparison with ARIMA, SARIMA, SARIMAX, LSTM, and CNN--LSTM models.

Taken together, the literature points to three main conclusions that motivate this project. First, classical studies show that macroeconomic structure matters, but they also warn that forecast gains may not survive across regimes~\cite{hamilton2003,kilian2009,baumeister2016}. Second, deep-learning studies suggest that nonlinear models can help, though their gains are often dataset-specific and not always benchmarked against strong classical baselines~\cite{cen2019,busari2021,kim2018}. Third, news- and event-based studies indicate that external narrative information is useful, but they rarely test its incremental value jointly with macroeconomic variables in a reproducible energy-forecasting setting~\cite{tetlock2007,li2014,shapiro2022,leetaru2013}.

Despite these advances, a unified benchmarking framework that simultaneously compares the isolated and joint contributions of macroeconomic and news-based signals --- using both test-set accuracy and temporal walk-forward backtesting --- remains absent from the literature. The present study addresses this gap by providing such a framework across two distinct energy commodities.

\section{Data}\label{secdata}

This study uses two main data sources: macroeconomic indicators and global news event data. The objective is to integrate structured macroeconomic signals with event-driven geopolitical information in a temporally aligned and methodologically consistent framework, thereby enabling a rigorous assessment of their respective and joint contributions to energy price predictability. Macroeconomic variables capture the broader economic setting, while news-based signals provide a partial view of real-world events, sentiment, and market stress. Using both sources together allows us to examine whether they contribute distinct and complementary information for energy-price prediction.

\subsection{FRED Data}\label{subsec:freddata}

The macroeconomic dataset is obtained from the Federal Reserve Economic Data (FRED) platform, which provides a centralized collection of economic time series compiled from official sources. We access the series through the FRED API so that variables with different frequencies can be collected and processed in a consistent way.

The selected macroeconomic variables are meant to represent broad channels through which energy prices may be affected, including industrial activity, inflation expectations, exchange-rate conditions, and financial-market uncertainty. These variables do not mechanically determine oil and gas prices, but they do summarize the economic environment in which demand conditions, investment decisions, and risk perceptions evolve.

\subsubsection{GDELT Data Processing}\label{subsubsec:gdeltprocessing}

We process the GDELT data to extract a compact set of signals from a very large event archive. The main steps include retaining only US-related events, one-hot encoding `EventCode', aggregating event counts daily, and computing summary measures such as average Goldstein Scale and average tone. When needed, these series are further aggregated to the monthly level. The result is a more compact and interpretable time-series representation of event activity. Procedure~\ref{alg:gdelt_processing} formalizes this transformation from raw event records to aggregated forecasting inputs.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{GDELT Data Processing and Feature Extraction}
\label{alg:gdelt_processing}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Raw GDELT event records $R$, target country $C=\text{US}$, target frequency $\text{freq}=\text{monthly}$
\Ensure Aggregated feature matrix $G_{\text{agg}}$ indexed by month
\State $R_{\text{US}} \gets \{r \in R : r.\text{Actor1CountryCode}=C \textbf{ or } r.\text{Actor2CountryCode}=C\}$
\State $\textsc{EventCategories} \gets \textsc{UniqueValues}(R_{\text{US}}.\text{EventCode})$
\ForAll{category $c$ in $\textsc{EventCategories}$}
    \State $R_{\text{US}}[\text{EventCode}_{c}] \gets \mathbb{1}[R_{\text{US}}.\text{EventCode} = c]$
\EndFor
\State $G_{\text{agg}} \gets \textsc{GroupByMonth}(R_{\text{US}})$
\Statex \hspace{1.5em}$\textsc{EventCount} \gets \sum \text{NumMentions}$
\Statex \hspace{1.5em}$\textsc{AvgGoldstein} \gets \text{mean}(\text{GoldsteinScale})$
\Statex \hspace{1.5em}$\textsc{AvgTone}_{\text{monthly}} \gets \text{mean}(\text{AvgTone})$
\Statex \hspace{1.5em}$\textsc{EventCodeCounts} \gets \sum (\text{EventCode}_{\ast}\text{ columns})$
\ForAll{category $c$ in $\textsc{EventCategories}$}
    \State $G_{\text{agg}}[\text{EventCode}_{c}] \gets G_{\text{agg}}[\text{EventCode}_{c}] / G_{\text{agg}}[\text{EventCount}]$
\EndFor
\State \Return $G_{\text{agg}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Data Integration}\label{subsubsec:dataintegration}

We then merge the processed macroeconomic and GDELT datasets on a common date index. We retain only the overlapping sample so that all model inputs remain temporally aligned.

At this point, the paper moves from data construction to model-design choices. The next section explains how the aligned dataset is partitioned, transformed, and evaluated so that the later empirical comparisons remain consistent across classical and neural forecasting approaches.

\section{Methodology}\label{secmethod}

This section describes the forecasting workflow applied after the FRED and GDELT sources are integrated. We first define the temporal splitting strategy, then outline the preprocessing and stationarity checks used to prepare the inputs, and finally present the classical and neural models together with the evaluation framework. Organizing the pipeline in this order keeps the later results directly connected to the design choices that produced them.

\subsection{Key Design Principles}\label{subsec:keydesign}

The pipeline follows a few simple design principles. Strict adherence to temporal ordering is enforced at every stage of the pipeline to preserve the causal structure of the time series and to prevent any form of look-ahead bias that would artificially inflate model performance. In accordance with the no-future-information constraint fundamental to out-of-sample forecasting evaluation, all models are trained exclusively on observations preceding the forecast horizon, with strict enforcement applied across both the classical and neural network model classes. Data leakage is avoided throughout, and the preprocessing logic is kept as consistent as possible across model families so that the comparisons remain fair.

Overall, this preprocessing pipeline provides a consistent basis for integrating macroeconomic indicators and news-based signals while keeping the later model comparisons interpretable.

\subsection{Stationarity Testing}\label{subsec:stationarity}

We test for stationarity before fitting the classical time-series models, since ARIMA-type specifications assume that the mean and variance structure of the modeled series is sufficiently stable over time. To do this, we use both the Augmented Dickey--Fuller (ADF) test and the Kwiatkowski--Phillips--Schmidt--Shin (KPSS) test. The ADF test takes the null hypothesis that the series contains a unit root and is therefore non-stationary, while the KPSS test reverses the perspective and treats stationarity as the null. Using both tests provides a more balanced diagnostic before model fitting. Throughout the analysis, we use a significance level of $\alpha = 0.05$. The ADF regression is given by
\begin{equation}
 \Delta y_t = \alpha + \beta t + \phi y_{t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^{p} \gamma_i \Delta y_{t-i} + \epsilon_t,
\label{eq:adf}
\end{equation}
The KPSS test statistic is computed as
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{KPSS} = \frac{1}{T^2 \hat{\sigma}^2} \sum_{t=1}^{T} S_t^2,
\label{eq:kpss}
\end{equation}
where $S_t = \sum_{i=1}^{t} e_i$ denotes the cumulative partial sum of the residuals from the regression used in the KPSS procedure, $T$ is the sample size, and $\hat{\sigma}^2$ is the long-run variance estimate. In Equation~\ref{eq:adf}, $\alpha$ is a constant, $\beta t$ is a deterministic trend term, $\phi$ is the coefficient on the lagged level term $y_{t-1}$, $p$ is the lag order, and $\epsilon_t$ is a white-noise error. When the ADF null cannot be rejected or the KPSS null is rejected, the series is differenced and tested again until stationarity is obtained. The required number of differences then determines the integration order $d$ in the corresponding ARIMA$(p,d,q)$ specification. In practice, the level series generally did not satisfy the combined stationarity criteria, but the transformed series used for classical modeling satisfied both tests at the 5\% level after differencing.

To complement the formal stationarity diagnostics, we also inspect seasonal-trend decomposition using Loess (STL) for both target series. STL separates each monthly price series into trend, seasonal, and residual components, which provides a visual diagnostic of recurring within-year structure alongside slower-moving medium-run dynamics. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:stl_decomposition}, both WTI crude oil and Henry Hub natural gas exhibit nontrivial seasonal structure, which helps justify the inclusion of seasonal differencing and seasonal lag terms in the SARIMA and SARIMAX specifications.

\subsection{Modeling}\label{subsec:modeling}

This study uses both classical statistical models and neural network-based approaches so that different aspects of the data can be evaluated under the same framework. Classical models serve as transparent baselines, while neural models are included to capture possible nonlinear relationships, interactions among variables, and longer temporal dependencies.

\subsubsection*{4.5.1 Classical Modeling}\label{subsubsec:classicalmodeling}

\subsubsection*{4.5.1.1 ARIMA}

In ARIMA, the model first transforms the time series into a stationary form through differencing. Then, for each time step $t$, the model computes the predicted value by combining a weighted sum of previous observations and previous error terms, as defined by,
\begin{equation}
 y_t = c + \sum_{i=1}^{p} \phi_i y_{t-i} + \sum_{j=1}^{q} \theta_j \epsilon_{t-j} + \epsilon_t.
\label{eq:arima}
\end{equation}
Here, the algorithm iteratively learns coefficients $\phi_i$ and $\theta_j$, which determine how much influence past values and past errors have on the current prediction. This makes ARIMA effective for modeling linear temporal dependencies in univariate data.

\subsubsection*{4.5.1.2 SARIMA}

SARIMA follows the same process as ARIMA but extends it by incorporating seasonal patterns. After differencing both regular and seasonal components, the model applies seasonal autoregressive and moving average terms:
\begin{equation}
 \Phi_P(B^s)\phi_p(B)(1-B)^d(1-B^s)^D y_t = \Theta_Q(B^s)\theta_q(B)\epsilon_t.
\label{eq:sarima}
\end{equation}
In this formulation, the algorithm represents repeating cycles by applying operations at lag intervals defined by the seasonal period $s$. This allows the model to account for periodic fluctuations observed in energy price data.

\subsubsection*{4.5.1.3 SARIMAX}

SARIMAX extends SARIMA by integrating external variables into the prediction step. At each time step, the model not only considers past values and errors but also includes the contribution from exogenous variables:
\begin{equation}
 y_t = c + \sum_{i=1}^{p} \phi_i y_{t-i} + \sum_{j=1}^{q} \theta_j \epsilon_{t-j} + \beta X_t + \epsilon_t.
\label{eq:sarimax}
\end{equation}
Here, the algorithm learns an additional set of coefficients $\beta$ that quantify the influence of macroeconomic and news-based features $X_t$. This enables the model to incorporate real-world drivers of price movement, making it more suitable for multivariate forecasting.

\subsubsection*{4.5.2 Neural Network-Based Modeling}\label{subsubsec:neuralmodeling}

Neural network models learn patterns directly from data by optimizing weights through iterative training. Instead of explicitly defining relationships, they approximate complex mappings between input sequences and outputs.

\subsubsection*{4.5.2.1 LSTM}

In LSTM, the model processes the input sequence step by step, where each input $x_t$ and previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$ are used to update the memory cell. At each step, the algorithm computes gate values to control information flow:
\begin{align}
 f_t &= \sigma\left(W_f[h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_f\right), \nonumber \\
 i_t &= \sigma\left(W_i[h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_i\right), \nonumber \\
 o_t &= \sigma\left(W_o[h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_o\right), \nonumber \\
 \tilde{C}_t &= \tanh\left(W_c[h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_c\right).
\label{eq:lstm_gates}
\end{align}
The cell state is then updated as
\begin{equation}
 C_t = f_t \cdot C_{t-1} + i_t \cdot \tilde{C}_t,
\label{eq:lstm_cell}
\end{equation}
and the hidden state is computed as
\begin{equation}
 h_t = o_t \cdot \tanh(C_t).
\label{eq:lstm_hidden}
\end{equation}
This step-by-step process allows the model to selectively retain or forget information across time steps. Finally, the last hidden state is passed through a linear layer,
\begin{equation}
 \hat{y} = W h_t + b,
\label{eq:lstm_output}
\end{equation}
to generate the forecast output. This mechanism enables LSTM to model long-term dependencies in sequential data. In implementation, the LSTM receives input tensors of shape $(\text{batch}, \text{time}, \text{features})$ and maps the final hidden representation to a continuous forecast through a linear output layer, making it suitable for nonlinear multivariate sequence learning.

\subsubsection*{4.5.2.2 CNN--LSTM}
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includesvg[width=0.95\linewidth,inkscapelatex=false]{prism-uploads/cnn_lstm_architecture_minimal}
\caption{CNN--LSTM pipeline.}
\label{fig:cnn_lstm_architecture}
\end{figure}
The CNN--LSTM model follows a two-stage process. First, the input sequence is passed through a convolutional layer,
\begin{equation}
 z = \mathrm{Conv1D}(x),
\label{eq:conv1d}
\end{equation}
which extracts local temporal features by applying filters across time steps. This transforms the input into feature maps that highlight short-term patterns. Next, these extracted features are passed into the LSTM layer,
\begin{equation}
 h_t = \mathrm{LSTM}(z_t),
\label{eq:cnn_lstm}
\end{equation}
where the LSTM processes the sequence of feature maps to model long-term dependencies. Finally, the output is computed using
\begin{equation}
 \hat{y} = W h_t + b.
\label{eq:cnn_lstm_output}
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:cnn_lstm_architecture} shows the CNN--LSTM architecture used in this study This combination allows the model to first identify local structures and then learn their evolution over time, making it particularly effective for complex time series influenced by multiple factors. As with the LSTM, the CNN--LSTM operates on tensors of shape $(\text{batch}, \text{time}, \text{features})$, with the final recurrent representation passed to a linear layer for continuous-value prediction.

The model-training and hyperparameter-selection routine for this architecture is formalized in Procedure~\ref{alg:cnn_lstm_optuna}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{CNN--LSTM Training and Hyperparameter Selection}
\label{alg:cnn_lstm_optuna}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Training sequences $(X_{\text{seq}}, Y_{\text{seq}})$ of shape $(N,k,F)$; number of Optuna trials $T=30$; validation split $\rho=0.1$
\Ensure Selected CNN--LSTM model $m^{\ast}$ and hyperparameter vector $\theta^{\ast}$
\State Define search space $\Theta$: convolution filters $\in \{32,64\}$, kernel size $\in \{2,3,5\}$, LSTM units $\in \{32,64\}$, dropout rate $\in [0.1,0.3]$, $L_2$ regularization $\in [10^{-4},10^{-2}]$, learning rate $\in [10^{-4}, 3 \times 10^{-3}]$, and batch size $=32$

    \State Compile $m_t$ with the Adam optimizer and Huber loss
    \State Train $m_t$ on $(X_{\text{seq}}, Y_{\text{seq}})$ using validation split $\rho$ and early stopping based on validation loss

\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection*{4.5.4 Evaluation and Backtesting}\label{subsubsec:evaluation}

We evaluate model performance using three standard error measures: Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE). RMSE penalizes large errors more strongly and is useful when large forecast deviations are especially undesirable. MAE provides an interpretable average magnitude of the forecast error. MAPE expresses the error in percentage terms, which helps compare model performance across crude oil and natural gas despite their different price scales.

The metrics are defined as follows:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}(y_t - \hat{y}_t)^2},
\label{eq:rmse}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MAE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left|y_t - \hat{y}_t\right|,
\label{eq:mae}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MAPE} = \frac{100}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left|\frac{y_t - \hat{y}_t}{y_t}\right|.
\label{eq:mape}
\end{equation}
To assess whether observed differences in forecast accuracy between model pairs are statistically significant rather than attributable to sampling variability, the Diebold--Mariano (DM) test~\cite{diebold1995} is applied. Let the loss differential be defined as
\begin{equation}
 d_t = L(e_{1t}) - L(e_{2t}),
\label{eq:dm_loss}
\end{equation}
where $L(\cdot)$ is a loss function and, in this study, squared error is used. The DM statistic is then given by
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{DM} = \frac{\bar{d}}{\sqrt{\hat{V}(\bar{d})/n}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1),
\label{eq:dm_stat}
\end{equation}
under the null hypothesis of equal predictive accuracy. Under the sign convention in Eq.~\ref{eq:dm_loss}, a negative DM statistic indicates that the first-listed model has lower average loss than the second-listed model, whereas a positive statistic favors the second-listed model. Rejection of $H_0$ at the 5\% level ($p < 0.05$) provides formal statistical evidence that one model forecasts significantly better than another.

Procedure~\ref{alg:dm_test} summarizes the sequence of computations used to estimate the Diebold--Mariano test statistic and corresponding significance decision.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Pairwise Diebold--Mariano Predictive Accuracy Test}
\label{alg:dm_test}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Forecast errors $e_1=\{e_{1,1},\ldots,e_{1,n}\}$, forecast errors $e_2=\{e_{2,1},\ldots,e_{2,n}\}$, loss $L(e)=e^2$, significance level $\alpha=0.05$
\Ensure DM statistic, $p$-value, and significance decision
\State $n \gets \textsc{length}(e_1)$
\For{$t = 1$ to $n$}
    \State $d_t \gets L(e_{1,t}) - L(e_{2,t})$ \Comment{$d_t < 0$ favors Model 1}
\EndFor
\State $\bar{d} \gets \textsc{mean}(d_1,\ldots,d_n)$
\State $h \gets \lfloor n^{1/3} \rfloor$
\State $\gamma_0 \gets (1/n) \sum_{t=1}^{n} (d_t - \bar{d})^2$
\For{$\ell = 1$ to $h$}
    \State $\gamma_{\ell} \gets (1/n) \sum_{t=\ell+1}^{n} (d_t - \bar{d})(d_{t-\ell} - \bar{d})$
    \State $w_{\ell} \gets 1 - \ell/(h+1)$
\EndFor
\State $\hat{V} \gets \gamma_0 + 2 \sum_{\ell=1}^{h} w_{\ell}\gamma_{\ell}$
\State $\mathrm{DM} \gets \bar{d} / \sqrt{\hat{V}/n}$
\State $p\_\text{value} \gets 2(1 - \Phi(|\mathrm{DM}|))$
\If{$p\_\text{value} < \alpha$}
    \If{$\bar{d} < 0$}
        \State decision $\gets$ ``Model 1 is significantly more accurate''
    \Else
        \State decision $\gets$ ``Model 2 is significantly more accurate''
    \EndIf
\Else
    \State decision $\gets$ ``No significant difference''
\EndIf
\State \Return $\mathrm{DM}, p\_\text{value}, \text{decision}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

To formally evaluate directional forecasting capability --- a practically important property in commodity price applications --- we also compute Directional Accuracy (DA):
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{DA} = \left(\frac{1}{n}\right) \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbb{1}\left[(y_t - y_{t-1})(\hat{y}_t - y_{t-1}) > 0\right]
\label{eq:da}
\end{equation}
Here, $y_t$ denotes the observed value at time $t$, $\hat{y}_t$ denotes the predicted value, and $n$ is the number of forecast points in the evaluation sample.

In Equation~\ref{eq:da}, $\mathbb{1}[\cdot]$ is the indicator function, which takes value 1 when the predicted and actual price changes share the same sign and 0 otherwise. A value of $\mathrm{DA}=0.5$ is equivalent to a random directional guess, but values above 0.5 should still be tested formally before they are interpreted as genuine directional skill.

To distinguish descriptive directional performance from statistically defensible directional skill, we also evaluate each reported DA value with an exact one-sided binomial test against the random-direction benchmark
\begin{equation}
H_0: p = 0.5 \qquad \text{versus} \qquad H_1: p > 0.5,
\label{eq:da_hypothesis}
\end{equation}
where $p$ denotes the probability of correctly predicting the direction of change. If a model records $s$ correct directional calls out of $n$ evaluation points, the exact $p$-value is
\begin{equation}
p_{\text{binom}} = \sum_{j=s}^{n} \binom{n}{j} (0.5)^n.
\label{eq:da_binom}
\end{equation}
For the fixed holdout forecasts in this study, $n=5$, so only a perfect $\mathrm{DA}=1.00$ rejects the null at the 5\% level. For walk-forward backtests, $n$ equals the number of backtest forecast points implied by the rolling evaluation window, making the same test more powerful when the backtest contains more observations.

Accordingly, the results tables in Section~\ref{sec5} report DA alongside RMSE, MAE, and MAPE so that point forecast accuracy and directional performance can be interpreted jointly.

In addition to test-set forecasting, we use backtesting to assess temporal robustness. The main reason for using backtesting is that a single train--test split can provide an overly optimistic view of predictive performance when the selected test segment happens to be unusually favorable. In contrast, walk-forward backtesting evaluates the model across multiple rolling forecast origins, making it possible to assess whether performance remains stable under changing market conditions and different local regimes. In this study, backtesting means that we repeatedly train the model only on past data and then test it on later observations that were not seen during training. At each step, the training window contains historical observations up to a given time point, and we evaluate the model on the next unseen segment. We then move the window forward and repeat the process. This procedure is important in time-series forecasting because it reflects how the model would behave in a real forecasting setting, where future values are unavailable at training time.

Formally, let the full training history span $t = 1, \, \ldots, \, T$, let $m$ denote the minimum training-window size, and let $h$ denote the forecast horizon. The walk-forward backtesting procedure is defined for $k = m, m+1, \, \ldots, \, T-h$ as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Train the model on observations $\{y_1, \, \ldots, \, y_k, X_1, \, \ldots, \, X_k\}$.
    \item Generate forecasts $\hat{y}_{k+1}, \, \ldots, \, \hat{y}_{k+h}$.
    \item Record forecast errors $e_t = y_t - \hat{y}_t$ for $t \in \{k+1, \, \ldots, \, k+h\}$.
    \item Advance $k$ by one step and repeat the procedure.
\end{enumerate}

This procedure produces a sequence of out-of-sample errors across multiple temporal segments, enabling assessment of forecast stability across different market regimes and avoiding the over-optimism that can arise from a single train--test split. For the neural-network models, the backtest is implemented using the same sequence specification as the training stage: the input-window size and output-window size used during backtesting are identical to those used when constructing the training sequences, so the evaluation reflects temporal generalization rather than a change in sequence length or forecasting format.

Procedure~\ref{alg:walk_forward_backtest} formalizes the walk-forward evaluation routine used for the neural-network models in this study.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Walk-Forward Backtesting for Neural Models}
\label{alg:walk_forward_backtest}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Trained model $m$, time series $Y$, features $X$, minimum training window $m_{\min}$, forecast horizon $h$, input window size $k$
\Ensure Out-of-sample error sequence and backtest metrics
\State errors $\gets [\ ]$
\State $T \gets \textsc{length}(Y)$
\For{$\text{step} = m_{\min}$ to $T-h$}
    \State $Y_{\text{train}} \gets Y[1:\text{step}]$
    \State $X_{\text{train}} \gets X[1:\text{step}]$
    \State update $m$ on the current training window
    \State $\hat{Y} \gets m.\textsc{Predict}(Y_{\text{train}}[-k:], X_{\text{train}}[-k:], h)$
    \For{$j = 1$ to $h$}
        \State $e_{\text{step}+j} \gets Y[\text{step}+j] - \hat{Y}[j]$
        \State append $e_{\text{step}+j}$ to errors
    \EndFor
\EndFor
\State $\mathrm{RMSE}_{\text{bt}} \gets \sqrt{\textsc{mean}(\text{errors}^2)}$
\State $\mathrm{MAE}_{\text{bt}} \gets \textsc{mean}(|\text{errors}|)$
\State $\mathrm{DA}_{\text{bt}} \gets \textsc{DirectionalAccuracy}(Y, \hat{Y})$
\State \Return $\mathrm{RMSE}_{\text{bt}}, \mathrm{MAE}_{\text{bt}}, \mathrm{DA}_{\text{bt}}, \text{errors}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Backtesting therefore serves as a stricter evaluation than a single train--test split. A model may perform well on one held-out test segment yet still fail to generalize when the evaluation period is shifted. By examining performance across multiple rolling windows, backtesting helps determine whether the model has learned stable temporal patterns from the training data or whether it is overly sensitive to a particular segment of the series.

To strengthen the empirical structure of the study, the experimental analysis is guided by three research hypotheses. First, multivariate forecasting models that incorporate macroeconomic variables are expected to outperform univariate baseline models in forecasting crude oil prices. Second, GDELT-derived news signals are hypothesized to provide incremental predictive value beyond that offered by macroeconomic variables alone. Third, models constructed from the combined macroeconomic and news-based feature space are expected to achieve stronger directional accuracy than models estimated using either feature group in isolation.

\section{Experiments and Results}\label{sec5}

We organize the experiments to match the evaluation framework described in Section~\ref{subsubsec:evaluation}, so that we assess each forecasting setting with the same RMSE, MAE, MAPE, and walk-forward backtesting criteria. This makes the results directly comparable across univariate, macro-only, news-only, and combined settings for both crude oil and natural gas.

This section presents results for all fully completed experimental configurations, with reported RMSE, MAE, MAPE, and directional-accuracy metrics drawn directly from stored artifacts to ensure full reproducibility. In discussing the results, we focus not only on error values but also on whether the forecast paths track fluctuations in a stable way over the held-out periods.

\subsection{Univariate Modelling}\label{subsec:univariate_models}

The univariate models for crude oil and natural gas use only past values of the target series. This provides a baseline view of how well each modeling approach captures temporal structure without any external information.

We begin with ARIMA models. For crude oil, the selected specification is ARIMA $(0,1,1)$, and for natural gas it is ARIMA $(0,1,0)$. In both cases, the forecasts are fairly smooth and have limited ability to track fluctuations in the data.

We then introduce SARIMA models to account for seasonality. For crude oil, the specification is SARIMA $(1,1,0) \times (0,1,0,12)$, and for natural gas it is SARIMA $(0,1,0) \times (0,1,0,12)$. Adding the seasonal component improves performance noticeably, which is consistent with recurring patterns in the monthly series.

Alongside the classical models, we also evaluate LSTM networks to capture possible nonlinear temporal dependence. For crude oil, the best configuration uses an input window of 5 time steps and a single-step output, while for natural gas the best configuration uses an input window of 10 time steps and a single-step output. Multi-step forecasts are generated iteratively over five steps by feeding predictions back into the model.

Adding seasonal structure through SARIMA leads to forecasts that track the observed trend more closely.

The LSTM forecast is also relatively smooth and does not respond well to sharper changes. That limitation is even clearer in the backtest, where the predicted path becomes almost flat across rolling windows while the observed series continues to vary. The resulting RMSE and MAE values are much larger. Relative to the random-walk benchmark, SARIMA reduces crude-oil RMSE from 12.258 to 1.210 and raises directional accuracy from 0.00 to 0.60, which clarifies that the seasonal specification is outperforming a standard naive baseline rather than only weaker learned alternatives. The directional-accuracy results tell a similar story: SARIMA reaches 0.60, compared with 0.00 for the random walk, 0.20 for ARIMA, 0.50 for the LSTM forecast, and 0.10 for the LSTM backtest. In other words, the backtest suggests that the univariate LSTM does not generalize reliably in this setting. Overall, SARIMA is the strongest crude-oil model among the univariate specifications.

The Diebold--Mariano results require a more careful reading. Under the sign convention defined in Eq.~\ref{eq:dm_loss}, the statistically significant ARIMA--SARIMA comparison ($\mathrm{DM}=-2.31$, $p=0.021$) favors the first-listed model, ARIMA, rather than SARIMA. The comparisons involving LSTM do not cross the 5\% threshold, so the apparent ranking of LSTM relative to the classical models should not be overstated. The newly added random-walk benchmark is useful for contextualization even though it is not yet included in Table~\ref{tab:dm_univariate_oil}: if a future DM comparison were to show that SARIMA does not significantly outperform the random walk over this short five-month holdout, that would still be an important substantive result because it would indicate that much of the apparent gain is not statistically distinguishable from a naive carry-forward rule. Taken together, Tables~\ref{tab:univariate_crude} and~\ref{tab:dm_univariate_oil} suggest that the aggregate error metrics favor SARIMA, while the reported DM sign in the ARIMA--SARIMA row favors ARIMA under the manuscript's stated convention.

\subsection{Feature Contribution Analysis}\label{subsec:feature_contribution}

Having established that the combined setting is the most promising overall, the next step is to ask why it performs better. The combined-feature results therefore raise a natural interpretive question: which exogenous variables appear to drive the improvement in the SARIMAX specifications? For the oil combined-feature SARIMAX model, this can be examined directly through the estimated regression coefficients on the exogenous variables. Because the predictors are not all measured on the same scale, the discussion below treats coefficient magnitude as a local indicator of influence within the fitted model rather than as a fully standardized cross-variable effect size. Even with that caveat, the absolute coefficient values are still useful for identifying which retained macroeconomic and news-derived variables receive the largest weights in the combined linear specification.

Table~\ref{tab:feature_contribution_summary} reports the three largest macroeconomic coefficients and the three largest news-derived coefficients by absolute magnitude for the oil SARIMAX model. On the macroeconomic side, \textit{brent\_crude\_oil} is by far the strongest contributor ($\beta=21.0831$), which is economically intuitive because Brent and WTI prices move closely as benchmark crude series. The next-largest macro coefficients are \textit{real\_usd\_index} ($\beta=-1.1749$) and \textit{ind\_prod\_drill} ($\beta=1.0230$), which point respectively to currency-channel effects and domestic supply-side activity. On the news side, the largest absolute coefficients are associated with \textit{AvgTone} ($\beta=4.0160$), \textit{EventCode\_196} ($\beta=-5.0580$), and \textit{EventCode\_1723} ($\beta=-3.4462$). Taken together, these results suggest that the improvement of the combined SARIMAX specification is driven by a mix of persistent macro-financial structure and a smaller set of high-impact news signals that capture sentiment and specific event categories.

This coefficient pattern helps explain why the combined SARIMAX models improve over the isolated macro-only and news-only configurations. The macro variables contribute medium-run structural information, while the GDELT variables contribute event-sensitive shifts in stress, sentiment, and specific geopolitical event patterns. Their joint use therefore gives the linear model access to both persistent economic context and shorter-horizon news pressure. For the neural models, the present study does not retain a formal permutation-importance or SHAP analysis, so feature attribution remains less direct; adding such diagnostics would be a valuable extension because it would show whether the same variables dominate the nonlinear models as well.

\subsection{Hypothesis Evaluation}\label{subsec:hypothesis_evaluation}

After comparing model families and interpreting the combined-feature coefficients, we return to the paper's three central research questions. This subsection evaluates those hypotheses against the empirical evidence reported in Section~\ref{sec5}. The goal is to distinguish between full support, partial support, and lack of support in a way that is directly tied to the observed RMSE, MAPE, and directional-accuracy outcomes.

\subsubsection{Macroeconomic variables improve over the univariate baseline}
The evidence for this hypothesis is mixed rather than uniform across commodities and model classes. For crude oil, the univariate SARIMA model already performs strongly, with RMSE $=1.210$ in Table~\ref{tab:univariate_crude}. By comparison, the combined-feature SARIMAX model records RMSE $=1.235$, which is close in magnitude but does not constitute a clear improvement in point-forecast accuracy. At the same time, the multivariate neural models benefit more visibly from macroeconomic inputs than their univariate counterparts, especially in the crude-oil macro-only setting, where CNN--LSTM attains stronger directional performance than the simpler univariate neural configuration. Taken together, the macroeconomic variables appear useful in some specifications, but they do not consistently dominate the strongest univariate baseline across all cases.

\subsubsection{GDELT news signals add incremental value beyond macroeconomic variables alone}
For crude oil, the news-only setting does not improve point accuracy relative to the macro-only setting: the news-only CNN--LSTM produces RMSE $=12.459$, which is substantially worse than the best macro-only crude-oil result. However, the same news-only crude-oil configuration still reaches DA $=0.60$, which suggests some directional utility even when point forecasts remain weak. For natural gas, the standalone contribution of news signals is considerably weaker. In that setting, the news-only model yields very large percentage errors, with MAPE reaching approximately $113\%$, indicating that GDELT-derived features provide little reliable predictive value when used alone. Accordingly, the evidence supports only a limited incremental role for news features in crude oil and no meaningful standalone support for natural gas.

\subsubsection{Combined macroeconomic and news-based features yield stronger directional accuracy}
The combined-feature setting produces the strongest numerical directional results reported in the paper. For natural gas, the combined SARIMAX model achieves DA $=0.80$, while for crude oil the combined CNN--LSTM forecast also reaches DA $=0.80$, as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:summary_best_models}. These values exceed the directional performance observed in the isolated macro-only and news-only settings for the corresponding best-performing models. However, once the exact binomial significance test is applied, those five-point holdout DAs do not reject the null of random directional guessing at the 5\% level. Accordingly, the combined feature space appears promising for directional modeling, but the evidence for statistically genuine directional skill remains limited in the fixed-holdout forecasts.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec12}

The preceding results sections show a consistent pattern: model performance depends not only on the forecasting architecture, but also on how the external information is represented and combined. More broadly, predictive performance is strongly contingent on both the nature of the exogenous information incorporated and the commodity under examination --- a finding that reflects the fundamentally different price formation processes governing crude oil and natural gas markets. In the macro-only setting, performance remains uneven because broad economic indicators move more slowly than commodity prices. As a result, they are more useful for describing medium-run conditions than for explaining abrupt short-run moves. This structural mismatch between the low-frequency dynamics of macroeconomic variables and the higher-frequency movements of commodity prices helps explain why macro-only models can detect broad directional regime shifts while nonetheless failing to resolve the timing and magnitude of localized price shocks.

The news-only setting highlights a different trade-off. GDELT-based features do contain relevant event information, but the aggregation process also adds noise by compressing heterogeneous news into summary variables such as event counts, tone, and conflict intensity. For that reason, news-only models can sometimes pick up broad directional changes, but they remain unstable, especially for natural gas, where large price movements are hard to infer from aggregated event signals alone.

The combined macro-plus-news setting performs best because it brings together slower-moving structural context and faster-moving event information. Economically, that means the models can use both medium-horizon fundamentals and shorter-horizon signals related to geopolitical disruption, policy uncertainty, and changes in market sentiment. In this setting, SARIMAX appears to benefit the most, likely because many of the relevant transmission channels from macroeconomic conditions to monthly energy prices are relatively stable and close to linear over multi-year horizons. Variables such as industrial production, exchange rates, and aggregated news pressure may influence prices through demand conditions, inventory expectations, trade competitiveness, and risk premia in ways that accumulate gradually rather than immediately.

By contrast, LSTM and CNN--LSTM offer more representational flexibility, but that flexibility is not automatically an advantage in this setting. The sample is modest relative to the needs of neural architectures, the data are monthly rather than high-frequency, and the exogenous inputs are already aggregated into fairly smooth summary indicators. Under those conditions, there may simply be less nonlinear structure available for the neural models to exploit. Rather than uncovering additional signal, they may either overreact to local fluctuations or learn relationships that are too smooth to respond well when prices move abruptly. When the joint feature space is informative enough, the stronger performance of SARIMAX likely reflects the fact that medium-run energy prices remain linked to interpretable macroeconomic fundamentals and persistent event-driven pressures that a linear dynamic model can still capture reasonably well.

These patterns are broadly consistent with the prior literature, but they also sharpen it in a few ways. The comparatively strong SARIMA and SARIMAX results align with the caution in Baumeister and Kilian~\cite{baumeister2016} that relatively disciplined classical specifications can remain competitive when structural relationships are persistent and the sample is not especially large. At the same time, the uneven LSTM results are in line with application-oriented deep-learning studies such as Cen and Wang~\cite{cen2019} and Busari and Lim~\cite{busari2021}, which suggest that neural performance is sensitive to specification, training design, and the information content of the input sequence rather than uniformly superior. The finding that combined macroeconomic and news-based features outperform isolated feature groups also echoes the broader argument in the external-information literature --- including Tetlock~\cite{tetlock2007}, Li et al.~\cite{li2014}, and Shapiro et al.~\cite{shapiro2022} --- that market-relevant narrative signals are most useful when interpreted alongside structured economic context rather than in isolation.

Overall, the findings suggest that exogenous variables are most useful as complements to price history rather than as substitutes for it. Relative to prior work that often studies one commodity, one model family, or one external source at a time, the present comparison makes it easier to see where macroeconomic and news-based inputs help and where their effects remain limited. In this study, their clearest value is not perfect point forecasting, but better directional understanding, better turning-point detection, and a more interpretable view of which outside conditions coincide with changes in energy prices.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}\label{sec14}

Energy-price forecasting remains difficult because prices reflect a complicated mix of economic, geopolitical, and market-specific forces, many of which are not fully captured by the selected macroeconomic indicators and aggregated news signals. Although these inputs provide useful context, they may miss sudden shocks or localized events that drive sharp price movements. Classical time-series models are also inherently linear, while neural models, even though they are more flexible, often produce smoother outputs and may still struggle during periods of high volatility.

Another limitation pertains to the representational granularity of the news data. Aggregated GDELT features are computationally convenient, but they necessarily compress event-level detail and may therefore lose some of the richer information embedded in the underlying news stream. In addition, the noise and volatility of energy-price series, especially for natural gas, can affect model stability and make generalization uneven across time periods.

An additional methodological limitation is that we applied walk-forward backtesting only to the LSTM and CNN--LSTM models, whereas we evaluated the classical ARIMA, SARIMA, and SARIMAX specifications on a single fixed holdout split. This creates an asymmetric comparison, because we assessed the neural models under repeated rolling-window re-estimation while we judged the classical models under a one-shot forecasting setup. We did not include the classical models in the backtesting stage primarily to keep the experimental workload tractable across the large set of model-feature combinations and because we treated them as single-shot benchmark specifications after order selection and fitting. Even so, this design choice limits strict comparability across model classes and remains a methodological constraint of the present study.

Future work could address these limitations by expanding both the feature space and the modeling strategy. One natural extension is to test additional macroeconomic variables that may be only indirectly related to energy prices but still carry useful information. Future work could substantially improve the quality and specificity of news-based signals by replacing aggregated GDELT summary statistics with richer natural language representations --- such as transformer-derived sentence embeddings (e.g., BERT, FinBERT) --- applied directly to the source text of relevant news articles. It may also be useful to model the temporal persistence of events more explicitly, since the effect of a news shock is unlikely to disappear immediately. Finally, attention-based or transformer architectures may be worth exploring, although any gains would still need to be checked carefully against the size and frequency of the available data.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec13}

This study investigated the extent to which macroeconomic indicators sourced from the FRED database and event-driven news signals derived from the GDELT repository can meaningfully improve the forecasting accuracy and directional tracking of WTI crude oil and Henry Hub natural gas prices. The results suggest that both sources of information are useful, especially when they are used together in a multivariate framework. Across the experiments, the combined macro-plus-news setting generally performed better than the macro-only and news-only alternatives, which points to a genuinely complementary relationship between structured economic signals and event-driven information.

The findings also suggest that GDELT-based features and macroeconomic variables are more helpful for detecting broad market direction and turning points than for producing consistently precise point forecasts. In particular, the combined-feature SARIMAX models deliver the most reliable overall performance in this study, which suggests that the joint feature space contains explanatory relationships that are harder to recover when the inputs are separated. From an economic perspective, that result is consistent with the idea that monthly oil and gas prices continue to move with persistent macro-financial conditions, while aggregated news signals add timely information about stress and event risk. The neural models capture parts of the underlying dynamics as well, but their forecasting and backtesting behavior is less stable, especially when price changes are abrupt.

Taken together, our empirical results indicate that incorporating heterogeneous exogenous information materially improves directional energy price modeling and reduces forecast error in well-specified linear frameworks, while simultaneously confirming that precise short-term point forecasting of volatile commodity prices remains an open and non-trivial research problem. Energy markets are shaped by rapidly changing geopolitical events, structural breaks, and nonlinear interactions that our aggregated macroeconomic indicators and news summaries represent only partly. For that reason, we view the framework proposed here as a useful step toward richer predictive modeling rather than a complete solution.

This study contributes a structured comparative framework for assessing univariate and multivariate forecasting models under a common validation design, while demonstrating the analytical value of integrating structured macroeconomic indicators with event-driven news features. Within this framework, RMSE serves as an indicator of how closely a model reproduces realized price levels, whereas directional accuracy (DA) reflects how reliably the model captures the sign of period-to-period price changes. Taken together, these evaluation perspectives suggest that external information can strengthen both point-forecast performance and directional inference. At the same time, the results reaffirm that precise forecasting of highly volatile commodity prices remains inherently challenging and continues to warrant further methodological development.
